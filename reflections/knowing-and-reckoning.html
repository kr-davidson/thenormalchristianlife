Knowing and
Reckoning

✦

Heart Orientation, the Spirit's Revelation, and the Living Reality of Romans 6

About This Paper

This paper was developed through an extended theological exploration using artificial intelligence as a research, dialogue, and writing partner. The underlying questions, themes, theological concerns, and editorial direction originated through engagement with Scripture, Watchman Nee's The Normal Christian Life, Romans 6, and broader questions surrounding revelation, reckoning, sanctification, and heart orientation. The paper was generated through iterative dialogue, refinement, critique, and editorial oversight.

It should therefore be understood as an AI-assisted theological reflection rather than a traditional academic publication.

Preface

Preface

This paper grows out of a question that began simply enough: why does the truth of Romans 6 so often remain inert in the lives of those who believe it?

The question, it turns out, is not simple at all. It reaches into the nature of spiritual revelation, the relationship between the heart's orientation and the mind's capacity to receive truth, the difference between doctrinal location and living knowing, and the compound of conditions that simultaneously resist and complicate the journey from one to the other.

Watchman Nee's chapters on knowing and reckoning in The Normal Christian Life press this question with unusual force. His account of seven fruitless years of reckoning, followed by a single morning of genuine seeing, raises the question of what was different about that morning, and what the believer is supposed to do in the years before it arrives. The answer this paper proposes is that the missing element is not primarily methodological but moral: the condition of the heart determines the capacity for genuine revelation, and no account of knowing and reckoning is adequate that does not place the orientation of the heart at its centre.

✦   ✦   ✦
Section I

The Morning That Would Not Stay Morning

There is a moment Watchman Nee describes that many readers pass over too quickly. He is sitting at his desk in Shanghai, having spent years trying to reckon himself dead to sin and finding the exercise produce nothing. Years of sincere effort. Years of reading Romans 6:11 and attempting to make it true by the force of attention. And then, on an ordinary morning, something shifts. Not because he tried harder. Not because he found a better technique. Because he saw.

He ran downstairs. He grabbed a brother in the kitchen. He said, with what must have looked like alarming enthusiasm to a man washing dishes: Do you know that I have died?

The brother was puzzled. Nee was not. He had seen something that made the question feel like the most natural announcement in the world. The fact had always been true. The cross of Christ had always accomplished what it accomplished. Romans 6:6 had always said what it said. But now he knew it, not as a proposition requiring his mental maintenance but as a reality requiring his response.

What had changed was not the fact. What had changed was his relationship to it. And beneath that change in relationship lay something that the rest of the chapter only partially names: a reorientation of the heart toward the God who had spoken the fact into existence. This is where the reflection must begin, because without it the discussion of knowing and reckoning floats free of its proper foundation.

Section II

The Problem With Knowing We Don't Have

The chapters on knowing and reckoning in The Normal Christian Life circle around a problem that is easier to recognize than to resolve. There is a kind of knowing that everyone who has spent time in Scripture possesses, and there is another kind of knowing that many earnest believers have never had, and the gap between them is not bridged by more information.

The first kind of knowing is what we might call doctrinal location. I know where Romans 6 is. I know what it teaches. I can explain co-crucifixion with Christ in terms that would satisfy a theology examination. All of this is genuine and none of it is worthless. But it is not what Nee means by knowing.

The second kind of knowing is what happens when a truth that has been residing in the mind takes up residence in the life, when it ceases to be something I believe and becomes something I see, something that now has the quality of obviousness that the chair I am sitting on has. I do not maintain my belief in the chair by an act of will. I simply know it is there. Nee is reaching for something analogous in the spiritual domain, a knowing so settled and so perceptually immediate that reckoning follows not as an effortful act of self-persuasion but as the natural acknowledgment of what is plainly real.

But what Nee's account of these two kinds of knowing does not fully surface is that the movement between them is not only a cognitive event. It is a moral and affective one. The heart that is genuinely oriented toward God, that has been moved by the Spirit to love what God loves and to desire what God desires, is a heart that is positioned to receive revelation in a way that the heart still primarily oriented toward its own experience and self-knowledge is not. Jonathan Edwards, in The Religious Affections, argues precisely this point: that genuine spiritual perception requires a reorientation of the affections toward their proper object, and that without this reorientation the mind can accumulate doctrinal knowledge indefinitely without the truth ever becoming operative.1 The knowing Nee describes is not simply a cognitive upgrade. It is the fruit of a heart that has been turned.

Section III

The Orientation That Precedes the Seeing

This is the dimension that deserves more careful attention than it typically receives in discussions of Romans 6. John Owen, in The Mortification of Sin in Believers, observes that the Spirit's revealing work characteristically encounters resistance not only in the understanding but in the affections, and that this affective resistance is frequently the deeper obstacle.2 The person who cannot seem to move from doctrinal knowledge to living knowing is not always simply waiting for the Spirit to act. They may be in a condition of heart that is structurally misaligned with the reception of the very truth they are seeking.

Augustine's account of his own pre-conversion years in the Confessions is the most honest document in the theological tradition of what this misalignment actually feels like from the inside.3 He knew the truth. He desired, in some genuine sense, the transformation the truth promised. And yet he famously prayed: Lord, give me chastity and continence, but not yet. This is not hypocrisy in the straightforward sense. It is the condition of a heart that has been shaped by years of habitual loves oriented away from God, a heart that genuinely wills toward God and simultaneously wills away from Him, and that cannot resolve this conflict from its own resources.

Augustine's diagnosis is that the will is bound by its own disordered loves, and that no amount of correct understanding can break this bondage from within. The heart must be reoriented before the knowing becomes available, and the reorientation of the heart is itself a work of grace that precedes and enables the revelation given to the understanding. He writes in On the Spirit and the Letter that the function of the divine law is not to enable the will but to expose its bondage, driving the soul to cry out for the grace that alone can redirect its loves.4

Andrew Murray, in Abide in Christ, makes a related observation from a different theological tradition.5 He argues that the central problem in the Christian life is not primarily cognitive, not primarily behavioral, but volitional in the deepest sense: the will has not been fully surrendered to Christ, and this unsurrendered will generates all the downstream difficulties, including the inability to receive spiritual revelation. F.B. Meyer, in Christian Living, argues that there is almost always a specific point of deepest resistance, and that it is frequently not the obvious area of moral failure but something that presents itself as entirely reasonable, even as spiritually motivated, while functioning as the central hinge on which the heart's resistance turns.6

The heart must be turned before the truth can be seen. The turning is itself grace, and no account of knowing and reckoning is adequate that does not place this prior reorientation at its centre.

Section IV

The Fact That Does Not Wait for Our Perception

Nee is insistent on a point that should be heard before anything else is said about method or sequence or pastoral strategy. The fact of our death with Christ does not depend on our knowing it. It does not require our reckoning to make it real. It does not become more true when we have a profound experience of it, or less true when we have none.

When Christ died, something happened to the whole of humanity that was in Him. When the last Adam was crucified, all that was in the first Adam was taken to that cross, including us, whether we were born yet or not, whether we would ever understand it or not. John Murray, in his Commentary on Romans, makes the same point with characteristic precision: the union with Christ that Paul describes in Romans 6 is an objective reality grounded in the historical event of the crucifixion and resurrection, and the believer's reckoning is the acknowledgment of this reality rather than its production.7

This means that the project of knowing and reckoning is not the project of making something true. It is the project of coming to live in what is already true. The difference matters enormously, because it determines what kind of effort is appropriate and what kind is not.

Herman Bavinck, in Reformed Dogmatics, argues that the Spirit's sanctifying work addresses the whole person organically, and that the reception of truth about one's identity in Christ requires not only a heart opened by revelation but one that is genuinely positioned to receive what the understanding is being shown.8 The person whose heart is fundamentally oriented toward their own self-assessment, toward their accumulated self-knowledge, toward their experiential history as the primary measure of reality, will find that the objective fact of their death with Christ remains at the level of doctrine precisely because the heart's orientation is pulling in the opposite direction from what the doctrine declares.

Section V

Why Reckoning Without Knowing Fails

Nee's account of his years of fruitless reckoning is not an argument against reckoning. It is an argument against a particular misunderstanding of what reckoning is. He tried, for years, to use Romans 6:11 as a formula for producing a state of deadness to sin that he did not actually possess in experience. He told himself he was dead. He told himself this repeatedly and with increasing determination. And the sin kept moving.

The problem was not the verse. The problem was the direction of the effort. He was trying to make himself believe something in the hope that the believing would produce the reality, rather than believing something because the reality was already there to be believed. He was, in his own honest account, using reckoning as a form of self-hypnosis, attempting to generate through mental repetition a conviction that could only ever be received, not manufactured.

But there is a layer beneath this that Nee does not fully excavate. D. Martyn Lloyd-Jones, in his Romans sermons on chapter 6, observes that the believer who finds reckoning persistently fruitless is typically a believer whose attention is still primarily fixed on their own interior condition rather than on the God who has made the declaration.9 The attention is directed inward, toward the self that is supposed to be dead, rather than outward and upward toward the Christ in whom the death has occurred.

Reckoning is not the engine of the process. It is the natural motion of a heart that has been turned toward God and a mind that has therefore seen something real.

This is a failure mode that is still very much alive. The believer who reads Romans 6 and then attempts to sustain, through disciplined self-reminding, the conviction that they are dead to sin, will typically find the exercise exhausting and ultimately fruitless. Not because the truth being affirmed is false but because the exercise misunderstands both what the affirmation is for and what condition of heart makes it possible. When the heart orientation comes first, the seeing follows, and when the seeing comes, the reckoning follows easily, the way I count my money accurately when I can see it clearly.

Section VI

The Gift That Cannot Be Earned and the Responsibility That Cannot Be Avoided

Here the reflection must hold two things together that are genuinely in tension, because both are true and neither can be abandoned without distorting the picture.

The first is that the revelation Nee describes is a gift of the Spirit. It cannot be manufactured. It cannot be earned by sufficient earnestness or achieved by the right combination of spiritual practices. The morning in Shanghai came when it came. Nee had been seeking for seven years. The revelation arrived when the Spirit gave it, not as the reward for seven years of seeking but as the gift that the seven years of seeking had not been able to produce and yet had not been wasted in pursuing.

Paul's prayer in Ephesians 1:17–18, that God would give the believers a spirit of wisdom and revelation in the knowledge of Him, having the eyes of your hearts enlightened, is a prayer worth taking with full seriousness. It acknowledges that the knowing that matters is a gift, and it positions the believer appropriately as one who asks rather than one who achieves.

But this receptivity is not passive. Lloyd-Jones, in Spiritual Depression: Its Causes and Cures, identifies as one of the most common obstacles to spiritual breakthrough the condition of a heart that is simultaneously seeking revelation and maintaining a prior commitment to its own experience as the final arbiter of what is real.10 The reorientation required involves a genuine turning away from the self as primary reference point and a genuine turning toward the God who speaks the definitive word.

The second thing that must be held is that Romans 6:11 is a command. Paul does not say: once the Spirit has brought you revelation, reckon. He says: reckon. This command assumes that the believers can comply with it, not because they can manufacture genuine faith by an act of will, but because faith's proper act of reckoning is precisely what they are called to exercise on the basis of what God has declared, even when feeling and experience do not yet confirm the declaration. The act of reckoning, genuinely and trustingly counting as true what God has said, is one of the primary means through which the Spirit brings revelation. It is an exercise performed from a particular orientation of heart: one trusting the external Word rather than monitoring the internal state.

Section VII

The Shapes That Knowing Takes

One of the things the chapters do not quite say, but which the broader theological tradition helps us see, is that the knowing Nee describes does not arrive in only one form. His own experience was dramatic and dateable. He could tell you the morning, probably the approximate hour, certainly the room. And his account of this experience has functioned, for generations of readers, as an implicit template, the thing to be sought, the form in which the breakthrough is expected to come.

But the Spirit distributes revelation with sovereign freedom. For some, it does come in the form Nee describes, a definite moment, a clear before and after. For others, the truth of Romans 6 moves from doctrinal location to operative conviction by a different path, gradually and almost imperceptibly, through accumulated engagement with Scripture, through suffering that makes abstract truth suddenly concrete, through the witness of another believer in whom the truth is visibly alive, through years of faithful reckoning that seemed to produce nothing until one day it became apparent that something had shifted without a single identifiable moment of shifting.

Lloyd-Jones addresses this directly in his sermons on Romans, distinguishing between the direct and indirect operations of the Spirit.11 Lloyd-Jones is quite insistent that both are genuine and that neither should be treated as the norm by which the other is judged deficient.

What both forms share, however, is the same underlying movement of the heart. Whether the revelation comes suddenly or gradually, what it reveals is not primarily a new doctrine about Romans 6 but a new orientation toward the God of Romans 6. Edwards makes this point in The Religious Affections by arguing that the mark of genuine spiritual revelation is not its phenomenological intensity but its orientation: genuine revelation always produces a greater love for God, a greater awareness of His beauty and sufficiency, a heart more genuinely turned toward Him rather than toward itself.12

Section VIII

The Strands That Complicate the Journey

What Nee's framework does not fully account for, and what pastoral experience consistently demonstrates, is that the journey from doctrinal location to operative knowing is rarely a simple linear movement interrupted only by the absence of revelation. It is more often a movement through a compound of simultaneously operating conditions, each real, each requiring attention, none of which can be fully addressed without considering the others.

There is the person who has never consciously encountered the truth of co-crucifixion with Christ. There is the person who understands it well and is genuinely seeking revelation. There is the person who understands it and seeks it but who carries, at a level below conscious inspection, the functional conviction that it cannot really be true for them, that their particular history or pattern of failure puts them in an exception class that the general declaration does not reach.

Owen, in The Nature, Power, Deceit and Prevalency of Indwelling Sin, argues that sin characteristically presents its resistance not as rebellion but as theological scruple or personal humility.13 The person who says I cannot believe this is really true for me is not typically experiencing simple cognitive confusion. They are frequently in the grip of a disordered heart orientation that has clothed itself in the language of spiritual realism. Addressing it requires not primarily better argument but a genuine confrontation with the question of whether the heart is willing to let God's word be more true than its own experience.

There is also the person whose disobedience in a specific area has created a spiritual dullness that prevents the reception of further light. Owen addresses this directly in Mortification of Sin, arguing that habitual disobedience creates a disposition of heart that is functionally incompatible with receiving revelation.14 And there is the person whose very earnestness has become a trap, whose intense focus on their own interior condition has made genuine outward-directed faith almost impossible. Lloyd-Jones describes this in Spiritual Depression as the paralysis of analysis.15

The strands are entangled not because several independent things are happening at once, but because they are all expressions of a single deeper condition: the heart that has not yet been fully turned toward God.

Bavinck's observation that these strands typically occur simultaneously rather than sequentially is important here.16 A person in the compound condition is not simply experiencing several discrete problems that can be addressed in order. They are in a condition of holistic misalignment in which the heart's fundamental orientation is generating multiple surface manifestations. Meyer's insight in The Secret of Guidance that there is almost always a specific, identifiable point of deepest resistance in the heart is a practically useful guide to where pastoral attention should ultimately arrive.17

Section IX

What the Community Carries

One thing that both Nee's account and most individual reflection on it underemphasizes is the role of the community in mediating the very knowing that is being sought. Nee ran downstairs and grabbed a brother. The brother was puzzled, which in the story serves as the comic foil to Nee's revelation. But there is something in that moment worth staying with.

The Spirit's revealing work is not only private. It occurs in preaching, in fellowship, in the mutual witness of believers to one another, in worship that is genuine encounter rather than performance. Gordon Fee, in God's Empowering Presence, argues that the Reformed and Keswick traditions alike tend to read Romans 6 through a framework of individual spiritual psychology that Paul did not share.18 For Paul, the Spirit is the eschatological gift of the new age, poured out on the community, and whose operation is characteristically communal before it is individual and cognitive.

Edwards's observation in The Religious Affections that reliable discernment of one's own spiritual condition is extraordinarily difficult is relevant here.19 The very faculties needed for accurate self-assessment are affected by whatever condition is being assessed. What the community provides, when it is functioning as it should, is the external reference point that private reflection cannot supply.

Jack Hayford's observation that genuine worship is one of the primary contexts in which contrary beliefs are overcome is relevant here not as a technique but as a description of what happens when the community genuinely encounters God together.20 The heart that has been oriented toward its own experience as the primary measure of reality finds it difficult to maintain that orientation in the presence of genuine worship, because genuine worship presents God as He actually is, and His actual character makes the self-referential orientation obviously inadequate.

Section X

The Restlessness That Is Already Grace

There is a final word that Augustine's perspective contributes to this reflection, and it is perhaps the most pastorally important of all.

The person who has read Nee's chapters on knowing and reckoning and found themselves moved by them, who has recognized in the description of fruitless reckoning their own experience, who has felt the longing for the kind of knowing that makes reckoning natural, who has prayed for the revelation Nee describes and not yet received it in the form he describes, that person is not outside the process these chapters are pointing toward. They are already in it.

The very longing is the Spirit's work. Augustine writes at the opening of the Confessions that our heart is restless until it rests in Thee, and this restlessness is not the problem to be solved before the journey can begin.21 It is the evidence that the journey has already begun, that the grace that reorients the heart has already made its move, and that the heart's dissatisfaction with its current condition is the Spirit's refusal to let it settle for less than its proper end.

Owen's account of how the Spirit works on the heart in Pneumatologia suggests that this partial reorientation is not a halfway house to be grown impatient with but a genuine work of grace to be received with gratitude and cooperated with faithfully.22 The Spirit does not typically reorient the heart all at once. He works organically, addressing the whole person simultaneously through a process that resists the imposition of a simple timeline.

That morning in Shanghai was real. The brother in the kitchen witnessed something genuine. But the seven years before it were not wasted. They were the Spirit's long work of reorienting a heart, not only bringing revelation to a mind, preparing not just the understanding but the whole person for a moment that could not have been forced and could not have been avoided. The moment when the fact that had always been true became, at last, something seen was also the moment when a heart that had been in the process of being turned finally arrived at the orientation from which genuine seeing becomes possible.

The knowing that Nee describes, and the reckoning that follows naturally from it, are the fruit of a heart that has been turned toward God. The turning is grace. The fruit is grace. And the restlessness that refuses to let us stop short of the fruit is, from the first moment of its appearing, grace as well.

Soli Deo Gloria
